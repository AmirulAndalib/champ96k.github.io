A while ago I spent a few days reconnecting with old classmates and friends from school. It started as a simple plan to catch up, but it turned into something much deeper, a long, honest look at how we grow, how old perceptions stick, and how we sometimes outgrow the relationships that once defined us.

This post is not about anyone specific. It's about the lessons I took away from that experience and the way I've come to think about friendships, change, and closure. My goal here isn't to judge anyone. We are all shaped by our own journeys, and I'm simply sharing my own view.

## The Past Is Not the Whole Story

We all carry stories from our younger years. In school, it's easy to be labelled as a "dropout" or a "duffer" if you struggle academically or choose an unconventional path. But what people often don't realise is how much we can change and grow over time. Our past doesn't define us, and it certainly doesn't determine our future.

When I reconnected with these old friends, I noticed something: some of them still saw me through the lens of those school days. Not out of malice, I genuinely believe they meant well, but perceptions, once formed, are hard to let go of. It was a reminder that, sometimes, the version of us that lives in someone else's memory is years behind the person we've become.

That's not a criticism. It's simply what happens when people remember us the way we once were. The important part is that I didn't let that old image define how I saw myself.

## We Outgrow Some Friendships, and That's Okay

Here's the honest part of my view: not every friendship is meant to last forever. Some connections are for a season, and when both people are no longer growing in the same direction, the relationship quietly changes. That doesn't mean either person is wrong. It just means the distance between who we were and who we are now has grown too wide.

Walking away after that reunion was bittersweet. There was nostalgia for the memories we shared, but also a quiet realisation that our paths had diverged. I chose not to keep those friendships going, not from resentment, but because I've learnt that it's important to surround myself with people who appreciate who I am today, not just who I used to be.

There's no blame in that choice. It's simply a preference for growth over comfort.

## The Power of Meeting People in Person

During that trip, the real reason I had gone was to finally meet someone I'd only ever known through screens. For a long time, I built a version of this person in my head, from messages, from assumptions, from my own imagination. The version I imagined was so vivid that it felt real.

Meeting them face to face showed me how easily we romanticise people we've never truly known. The longing, the "what if", most of it lives in our own minds, not in the other person. It taught me one of the most valuable lessons I've learnt: we don't miss the person; we miss the idea we built around them.

Life is too short to hold on to misconceptions and missed opportunities. Meeting in person clears the fog. It lets you decide, honestly and finally, what to move forward with and what to let go.

## What I Actually Learned

If I had to condense that whole experience into the lessons that stuck with me, they would be these:

1. **Growth changes your circle.** As you evolve, some relationships evolve with you and others quietly fall away. Both are natural. Neither is a failure.
2. **People remember the version of you they knew.** That's not their fault, and it's not yours either. What matters is that you don't let an old label steer your present.
3. **The imagined version of someone is not the real person.** We project so much onto people we've only met through screens. Reality, once met, is often both simpler and better.
4. **Closure is something you give yourself.** You can't control how others see you or whether they change their minds. You can only decide what you're willing to carry forward.
5. **If someone doesn't share your values, it's okay to step away.** Not to prove anything, but for your own peace of mind. It was hard at first, but with time, it simply became part of life.

## A Personal Note on Letting Go

In the months after that experience, things were a little tough. I spent time wondering whether I truly didn't care anymore or whether I'd simply moved on. Navigating what was mostly a one-sided connection turned out to be one of the most exhausting things I've been through.

What I came to understand is this: when someone has decided something, no amount of effort from you will change their mind. It doesn't matter how convincing you are, the outcome stays the same. The kindest thing, both for them and for you, is to accept it and let go.

There's a line from reflecting on all of this that I keep coming back to: *"In your search, I found myself."* For me, that phrase captured everything. The whole journey, with its reconnections, its illusions and its goodbyes, wasn't really about anyone else. It was about me. It helped me understand what I want in my life, what I'm willing to accept, and what I need to move on from.

So I chose to close that chapter. Not from bitterness, but from a quiet determination to move forward on my own terms.

## My View on It All

Here's the honest summary of how I see it now:

Connections that fade are not proof of failure, they're evidence of growth. Every person we meet, whether they stay or leave, nudges us toward a clearer picture of ourselves. The people I once felt closest to and the people I barely knew all played a part in that journey.

If I could give anyone reading this one piece of advice, it's this: don't measure who you are by the memories other people have of you. Measure it by who you've become, the values you hold, and the direction you're choosing for yourself. Meet people in person when you can, let go of imagined versions of them, and be graceful about the friendships that outgrow themselves.
